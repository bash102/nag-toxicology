The NAG Greenpoint-Williamsburg ToxiCity Map is an interactive map of toxic “hot spots” in Greenpoint and Williamsburg. Data used to compile this map came from city, state and federal sources, guided by on-the-ground knowledge from local activists. Click through the data layers on the map to understand some neighborhood trends such as population density and asthma hospital visits, and locate some specific sites such as former NuHart Plastics factory and the Exxon oil spill. Sites that have already been remediated and sites that require future remediation are included on this map.

NAG hopes that this project will help the community better understand the environmental concerns in the area they live in and ultimately to improve the health and well-being of the community by motivating and empowering community members to be more engaged in their local governing and policy-making process.

Waste Transfer Stations

This layer shows waste transfer stations, scrap metal facilities, and recycling sorting facilities. A waste transfer station is a facility that receives solid waste from waste collection vehicles, and it is transferred to larger tractor-trailers or marine barges to be taken to a processing facility outside of the city. These types of facilities are typically associated with truck traffic dropping off or picking up waste or recycling, and contribute to air pollution and truck congestion in Williamsburg and Greenpoint. The Newtown Creek Wastewater Treatment Plant is also considered on this map for its daily transfer of wastewater sludge to a marine barge, and will be receiving truckloads of organic food waste to be processed at the plant.

Potentially Polluted Sites

Highly Regulated Sites: The EPA compiles sites that have regulation requirements through their Facility Registry Service. Facilities in this database must report to a variety of EPA programs, some as minimal as the Minor Air Facility Permit or as major as the Superfund program. There are over 1,000 facilities in Community Board 1 that have reported to the EPA at least once for any state or federal environmental programs. The sites shown in this map are the sites that have the largest burden of regulations to comply with, symbolizing potential for environmental risk. To access the full database, you can search by facility or geography or download the spatial data.

An (E) designation comes from rezoning to notify an environmental requirement on a particular lot. The (E) designations in this map are lots that are required to address potential hazardous material contamination or air quality concerns (high ambient noise level (E) designations were omitted from this map) if the lot undergoes new construction or change in land use. An (E) designation does not signify pollution, but rather the potential for exposure to hazard material contamination or pollutants in ambient air quality. More information can be found on the NYC Office of Environmental Remediation’s website.

Spills are cataloged by NYS DEC for all accidental releases of petroleum, toxic chemicals, gases, and other hazardous materials. You can search for spills in their records on the DEC website. Spills are categorized on this map as minor (smaller than 100 gallons) and major (100 gallons and greater) spills.

Polluted and Remediated Sites

There are Superfund and brownfield sites at the state and federal level. A brownfield site is a property where soil contamination exceeds standards set by NYS DEC (state) or US EPA (federal). These properties must be remediated before being redeveloped. Severely contaminated sites are designated as Superfund sites. This map includes all state and federally designated brownfield and Superfund sites, including those that have been remediated.

The Exxon-Mobil Oil Spill was a decades-long oil leak that was discovered in 1978. Exxon has already removed 12.5 million gallons of oil, of the estimated 17 to 30 million gallons. Remediation of this site is on-going and overseen by NYS DEC. The oil spill was drawn from a map created by Riverkeeper in 2004 found in the Brownfield Opportunity Area nomination report from 2012. More information can be found on the NYS DEC website.

The Meeker plume was found during the Exxon-Mobil Oil Spill investigation. The plume of chlorinated solvents (teatrachloroethene – PCE and trichloroethene – TCE), was found in soil, soil vapor and groundwater. These solvents typically come from dry cleaning and degreaser compounds. The investigation for the source of this plume is ongoing and overseen by the NYS DEC. The Meeker Plume was drawn from an online map by the Newtown Creek Alliance.

The NuHart plume is the remnants of the NuHart Plastics plants. Phthalate and TCE contaminate the site, making it a Class 2 State Superfund site, a “significant threat to public health and/or the environment requiring action.” The plume on this map was drawn from a report from April 2015 from NYS DEC.

Future Flood Risk

The New York Panel on Climate Change has projected what the flood plain will look like in a 100-year and 500-year storm (a storm that typically occurs once every 100 and 500 years, respectively). This map shows the projected flood plains for the 2050s, from the EJNYC Mapper data from the NYC Mayor’s Office of Climate & Environmental Justice.

Fine Particles (PM2.5) & Nitrogen Dioxide (NO2)

Fine particles (PM2.5) are tiny particles of soot, dust and smoke that can be breathed deep into the lungs, worsening asthma and heart and lung disease. Nitrogen dioxide (NO2) is a gas that comes mostly from burning fuel in vehicles, especially trucks, and in building boilers. These layers show 2024 annual average levels from the NYC Community Air Survey run by the NYC Health Department. The survey combines street-level air monitors with information about traffic, buildings and land use to estimate pollution across the city on a 300-meter grid. The levels are estimates for neighborhood-sized areas, not readings at a specific address, and the differences within Greenpoint and Williamsburg are small, so the colors are best read as relative levels within the area.

Cancer Risk from Air Toxics

This layer shows the U.S. EPA's estimate of the lifetime cancer risk from breathing toxic air pollutants, from its AirToxScreen 2020 assessment. A risk of 36 in a million means that if one million people breathed this air every day for 70 years, about 36 would be expected to get cancer from it. Greenpoint and Williamsburg are around 34 to 41 in a million, above the U.S. median of about 29 and similar to most of New York City. Most of the risk comes from formaldehyde, which forms in the air from other pollutants, and from small sources like solvents, commercial cooking, construction equipment and traffic. EPA's estimate leaves out diesel exhaust particles, which it considers a likely cause of cancer but cannot yet put a number on.

AirToxScreen is a screening tool built from computer models, not air measurements. EPA says it should not be used to determine any one person's risk, and the small differences between tracts here are within its margin of uncertainty, so the colors show relative position within the area. EPA publishes block-level results; this map averages them by census tract, weighted by population, as EPA does for counties. The respiratory hazard index in the popups was calculated from EPA's published concentrations using EPA's method, because EPA did not publish it for 2020.

Combined Sewer Overflows

Much of New York City's sewer system carries sewage and stormwater in the same pipes. When heavy rain overloads these pipes, a mix of untreated sewage and stormwater flows into the East River and Newtown Creek through combined sewer overflow (CSO) outfalls. This layer shows the outfalls along the neighborhood's waterways and how often each one overflowed in 2025, from the NYC Department of Environmental Protection's annual CSO report to the state. The counts are estimates from DEP's computer model of the sewer system, not meter readings, and they rise and fall with each year's rainfall.

Harbor Water Quality

The NYC Department of Environmental Protection samples the harbor's water through its Harbor Survey. This layer shows sampling stations in the East River and Newtown Creek, with recent levels of fecal coliform and enterococci bacteria, which indicate sewage pollution, and dissolved oxygen, which fish and other aquatic life need to survive. Station colors compare the 2023-2025 average bacteria level with New York State's fecal coliform standard of 200 per 100 mL; Newtown Creek misses it at every station. Low oxygen in English Kills and the East Branch is partly offset by DEP's aeration systems.

Lead Water Service Lines

A service line is the pipe that brings water from the street main into a building. Older service lines can be made of lead, which can get into drinking water. This layer shows, for each census tract, the share of properties whose service line is recorded as lead or as galvanized steel that needs replacing, from the NYC Department of Environmental Protection's service line inventory (January 2026). Most records come from old plumbing paperwork, so some lines marked lead may not be, and many properties have an unknown material. Tracts with only a few properties, such as parks and public housing campuses, can show misleading percentages. Property owners can look up their own address on DEP's service line map.

Asthma Rates

Residents of Williamsburg and Greenpoint experience higher instances of asthma due to proximity to truck routes and sources of air pollution. This layer shows the yearly rate of emergency room visits for asthma from 2013 to 2017, by neighborhood. This data comes from the New York State Department of Health’s Environmental Public Health Tracking program, which is based on hospital discharge records (SPARCS).

Health Conditions

This layer shows the estimated share of adults in each census tract who have asthma, COPD, cancer (other than skin cancer), coronary heart disease, diabetes or high blood pressure, who have frequent poor mental health, or who smoke. Pick a condition from the menu under the button. The estimates come from the CDC's PLACES project (2025 release, 2023 data). They are modeled estimates, not counts of diagnoses: the CDC combines a national health survey with each tract's population, age, race and income to predict local rates. They mostly reflect who lives in a tract, such as its age mix, rather than measured local exposures, and they are not adjusted for age.

Cancer Cases

This layer compares the number of people diagnosed with cancer from 2011 to 2015 with the number expected, from the New York State Department of Health's Environmental Facilities and Cancer Mapping data. Pick a cancer type from the menu under the button. The expected number is what the area would have if its residents got cancer at the same rates as New Yorkers statewide of the same age and sex. It does not account for smoking, other personal risk factors or income. Areas are census block groups; small block groups were combined by DOH to protect privacy.

Small areas have few cases, so their numbers swing up and down by chance. A single case can make a rare cancer look many times higher than expected. DOH outlined areas where cases were at least 50% higher or lower than expected and unlikely to be due to chance; on this map those areas have a thick outline. Even then, more cases than expected does not mean the cause is local pollution. Cancer develops over many years, people move, and most cancers have many causes. Across Greenpoint and Williamsburg as a whole, cancer cases were below the expected number in 2011-2015.

Median Household Income

Median household income is the middle income value, meaning half the population has an income above that amount, and half have an income below that amount. Median household income was $80,483 for New York City and $80,734 for the United States, according to the 2020-2024 American Community Survey.

Population Density

This data reflects population estimates from the 2020-2024 American Community Survey (ACS). ACS is a running sample of approximately 1 in 40 households in the U.S. This sample is taken each month over a 1, 3 or 5-year period. The longer the sample time, generally the higher reliability of the data due to a larger sample size. These maps use the 5-year estimate from 2020-2024, the latest available 5-year data.

Industrial History Walking Tour

Follow the points and paths on this layer to experience NAG’s Industrial History Walking Tour. The points of interest on this tour commemorate the neighborhood’s industrial legacy while bringing attention to the chemical contamination they have left behind, and its impact to the health and well-being of current residents. Find out more information about the tour.
